## MaceWard

*Grounded-theory interviews with 16 professional artists · [creators' vocabulary × stages mostly observable actions]*

Note on the source: The four category names follow Botella's naming. The original calls them Artwork Conception, Idea Development, Making the Artwork, and Finishing the Artwork and Resolution.

**`conception`**

The artistic process begins with the conception of the artistic work. The work is introduced by an idea or a more or less vague impression. As Mace and Ward state, 'the genesis of an artwork arises from a complex context of art making, thinking, and ongoing experience.'

**`development`**

During the development stage, artists structure, complete, and restructure the idea. They identify the work's development possibilities according to their ideas and feelings. Artists make implicit and explicit decisions by observing their work; this evaluation prompts them to question the ideas, expressions, metaphors, and analogies they wish to use — and those they prefer to abandon or put aside for future work. Trials, tests, and fiddling characterize this stage, allowing artists to form a more precise idea of the initial project.

**`realization`**

At this phase the work undergoes a transformation from a purely conceptual entity into a conceptual and physical entity. The artwork now takes on physical constraints that may or may not have been previously considered or foreseen. As the concept of the work informs its physical structure, the process of physically making the artwork influences the development of the concept, so that content and form inform each other in an advancing process of development. Making an artwork does not involve rendering a prethought idea; it involves a process of negotiation between the artist and the developing work.

**`finalization`**

Finalization is the resolution of the artistic work. The artist evaluates the production and decides that the work is finished — or alternatively, chooses to pursue it further, abandon it, postpone it, store it, or destroy it. If the artist considers the work successful and satisfying, they may exhibit it. Finalization implies a deliberate judgment about the work's completeness.

## Tinio

*Mirror model, inferred from paint-layer stratigraphy · [researchers' terminology × stages mostly observable actions]*

**`initialization`**

The conceptualization and physical translation of a creative idea and the development of the compositional structure of the artwork. During this first stage the artist explores an idea that he or she considers viable: artists generate a set of potential ideas, one is subsequently selected and initiated by exploring its various facets, and relevant information is gathered through research. As part of that exploration artists construct numerous sketches, initial physical manifestations of an idea, through which the artist uses visual artifacts to embody what has until then been present mainly in the mind.

**`expansion_adaptation`**

Development and adaptation of the initial structure of the artwork. This stage involves the development and fine-tuning of aspects of an artwork that were established during the initialization stage, including continued development of the underdrawing through addition, modification, and deletion of specific elements, and the introduction of such things as shading details. Changes at this stage are relatively small-scale and can be understood as responses to the demands of the evolving work: they are more like modifying already-conceived ideas than introducing something wholesale new.

**`finalizing`**

The conclusion and final refinement of specific details of the artwork. This stage may be considered as beginning when significant structural changes to the foundation of the artwork cease, a sign that the work is nearing completion. What characterises it pertains less to changes to the structural foundation and more to fine-tuned refinements involving color, texture, and other subtle manipulations, together with the specific steps the artist takes to prepare the artwork for presentation.

## Botella17

*Interviews with 28 art students, 17 stages · [creators' vocabulary × stages mostly internal processes]*

Note on the source: The 17 stages are quoted from Table 4 ("Stages retained"). Stage 14 is called Examination in Table 4, Judgement in Table 5 and judgment in the Discussion; stage 17 is Abandoning in Table 4 and withdrawal in the Discussion. Table 4's names are used here.

**`immersion`**

Impregnating oneself with the topic, defining the topic, listening to instructions

**`reflection`**

Brainstorming, understanding, visualizing

**`research`**

Searching for references, documents, artists, information

**`inspiration`**

An idea gradually formed, instinct

**`illumination`**

"Aha, there is an idea!", emergence of an idea

**`trials`**

Sketches, note-taking, testing, trying out

**`assembly`**

Mixing or assembling ideas together

**`ideation`**

Finding and exploring new ideas

**`selection`**

Choosing ideas

**`technique`**

Choosing a technique, a style, a typography

**`realization`**

Production, composition, concretization, cleaning up

**`specification`**

Refining and detailing

**`finalization`**

Adding finishing touches, correcting, ending

**`examination`**

Taking a step back and examining one's production

**`presentation`**

Explaining and justifying one's production

**`break`**

Letting everything rest, digesting, decanting

**`abandoning`**

Putting aside or abandoning an idea

## Wallas

*Armchair introspection · [researchers' terminology × stages mostly internal processes]*

Note on the source: Based on the 1926 publication, in the form in which it is usually cited, with brief explanatory notes.

**`preparation`**

The first stage, Preparation, is "the stage during which the problem was investigated in all directions" — the creator gathers information, studies the problem, and engages in deliberate, conscious work on it.

**`incubation`**

The second stage, Incubation, is "the stage during which he was not consciously thinking about the problem" — the mind works on the problem below the threshold of awareness; no deliberate effort is expended.

**`illumination`**

The third stage, Illumination, consists of "the appearance of the 'happy idea' together with the psychological events which immediately preceded and accompanied that appearance" — the 'Eureka' moment when a solution or insight suddenly emerges.

**`verification`**

The fourth stage, Verification, is when the idea or solution is tested, evaluated, elaborated, and refined — the creator assesses whether the illumination is valid and works out its details and implications.
